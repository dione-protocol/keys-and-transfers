## Has DIONE Protocol been audited?

Yes, twice, by the same firm, and the distinction between the two matters:

- **The chain audit** (December 2023) concerns the protocol software: consensus, state handling, the contract layer.
- **The wallet audit** (February 2026) concerns the application you install: key handling, encryption, signing, update verification.

An audit of one says nothing about the other, which is why both are cited. What an audit establishes is that a firm examined specific code on a specific date and found specific things; it does not establish that the software has no bugs, and no honest reading of any audit report claims otherwise.

The published page links Hacken's protocol audits index rather than a wallet-specific report URL. That is a documentation gap rather than a finding, and it is recorded as such in this pack's conflict register.

## Is DIONE Protocol registered anywhere?

Yes: Ontario, Canada, through the two entities and their incorporation dates. What that means in practice is narrower than people assume - registration establishes that a company exists and files, not that a product works or that a token is a good purchase.

The useful test is whether the entity named on the site matches the entity in the register. If a site selling you something names a company you cannot find, that is the signal worth acting on.

## Why is DIONE listed as a scam on some sites?

This is the question that most often produces anxiety, and the honest answer has several parts.

**Automated scanners flag structure, not intent.** Tools score tokens on liquidity depth, contract verification, holder concentration and age. A young asset with thin pools on one venue scores badly on those metrics, and the output is a warning label rather than a finding of fraud.

**Review sites are partly advertiser-supported.** Some sites that rank "is X a scam" monetise the question, and a scary headline earns more than a nuanced one.

**Name collisions are real.** Several unrelated firms and tokens use a name close to DIONE, and some of them have their own regulatory history. A warning about a namesake is not a warning about this project.

**Warnings sometimes describe genuine risk.** A token can be legitimate in the sense of existing and being developed while still being a poor purchase: thin liquidity, volatile price, uncertain demand. Those are investment risks, not fraud, and conflating the two helps nobody.

The productive response to a scam listing is to check the specific allegation. If it says the team is anonymous, check the entity. If it says the contract is unverified, check the explorer. If it says the site is a lookalike, check the domain against the one the project publishes - [Spot a Fake DIONE Site or Support Scam](https://github.com/dione-protocol/keys-and-transfers/blob/main/spot-a-fake-dione-site.md) covers what the fakes look like.

## Reviews in other languages

Turkish coverage exists, including pages the project publishes itself, and third-party Turkish reviews, videos and forum posts exist alongside them. Nothing in this pack vets third-party reviews in any language, and the honest framing is that a review is an opinion with a date.

What travels across languages badly is context: a figure quoted from a 2024 article about the ERC-20 token is describing a different instrument from the one trading now, and a translation rarely carries that caveat. Check numbers against the explorer rather than against a translated article.

## What would change the answer

A fair assessment names its own falsifiers. If releases stopped for a long period, if the explorer stopped producing blocks, if the entity ceased filing, or if an audit's findings were ignored while marketing continued, the picture would be different.

None of those is the current state, and stating them is the point: legitimacy is a claim about checkable things staying true, not a label awarded once.
